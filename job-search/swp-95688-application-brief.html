The qualification arithmetic

This is the whole game. State HR screens minimum qualifications mechanically before a hiring manager sees anything, so the only question that matters up front is whether the numbers add up on paper.

Experience required3 yrs
Credit — doctorate degree− 2 yrs
Left for you to evidence1 yr
The posting's exact wording: three years in program planning, water protection and conservation, or public health/environmental program coordination. Those are three separate doors — you only need to get through one, and the third is the one to aim at.

Settle this before you write a word

There is a real ambiguity in the substitution clause, and it decides whether you need one year of experience or three.

Read the three substitution sentences in order. A bachelor's degree counts “in public health, environmental science, or a closely related field.” A master's counts “in these fields.” Then the third sentence says only: “A doctorate degree may substitute for two years of experience” — with no field named at all.

Taken literally, any doctorate qualifies. Taken in context, the field restriction carries down from the sentences above it. Which reading the screener applies is the difference between needing one year and needing three. Don't guess, and don't let it be decided by someone else's default reading after the posting closes. Ask.

How to apply

  1. Create your account and find the posting

    Go to mn.gov/careers, choose View all Jobs, and search Job ID 95688. Under Agency, selecting Health Department filters to MDH. Apply through the Apply for Job button on the posting itself — there is no email application route. Stuck in the system: Careers Help Desk, 651-259-3637.

  2. Write the resume to the state's format, not to a normal standard

    The posting says it twice, in italics, because it is the most common reason good applicants get screened out: describe your experience in the areas listed, and give the beginning and ending month and year for every job held. “2019–2023” is not enough. “March 2019 – November 2023” is. Years of service are credited by arithmetic on those dates.

    Mirror the posting's own nouns — program planning, program coordination, stakeholder assistance, data analysis, reporting. A screener is matching language, not inferring capability.

  3. Claim the doctorate substitution in writing

    Put the degree in the education section with its conferral date, then state the arithmetic plainly somewhere the screener will see it: the doctorate substitutes for two of the three required years. Don't assume it gets applied automatically — make it impossible to miss.

  4. Build the remaining year out of what you've actually done

    Target the “public health/environmental program coordination” door, not the water one. Running a clinical practice genuinely involves coordinating health programs, regulatory and licensure compliance, patient and community education, and tracking outcomes for reporting — that is program coordination in a health context, and it should be described in those terms rather than as clinical care.

    Be honest with yourself about the strength of this. State HR often reads “public health program coordination” as population-level work rather than individual patient care, so this is the part of your application most likely to draw a challenge. That's exactly why the email in the previous section matters, and why the appeal right below is worth knowing about.

  5. Prepare one concrete story for each interview-assessed criterion

    Three further minimum qualifications are judged at interview, not on the resume. Have a specific example ready for each:

    • Helping stakeholders prepare and implement a plan — someone you walked through a document or protocol they then had to carry out.
    • Working across multiple internal and external partners — agencies, local government, community organizations, referral networks.
    • Planning and coordinating program activities, analyzing data, and applying the findings to program improvement, policy, or regulatory and funding reporting. This is where your Python and analysis work belongs — it is the only criterion of the three that explicitly asks for data, so lead with something you actually built and what changed because of it.

  6. Address the preferred qualifications too

    If a lot of applicants clear the minimums, the pool gets narrowed on preferred qualifications — so they function as the real filter. The five listed are: applying public health or environmental planning to drinking water work; knowledge of state and federal drinking water regulations; managing complex multi-stakeholder projects; professional writing and presenting; and building collaborative relationships across agencies and communities. You can speak credibly to the last three today. The first two are readable in an afternoon (see the primer below).

What happens after you submit

The state publishes its own process, and one step in it is worth knowing about in advance.

  • The agency first reviews applications against statute and bargaining unit contract requirements — current state employees and union bid rights can take precedence before open applicants are reached.
  • After the posting closes, resumes and screening questions are reviewed to determine who meets the minimum qualifications.
  • If you're found not to meet them, you get an email — and, in the state's own words, “applicants have the opportunity to appeal this qualification determination.” Given the ambiguity in the doctorate clause, this is a genuine second chance rather than a formality. Note the date if it happens and respond promptly.
  • Those who clear go to the hiring manager, who may narrow further on preferred qualifications, then interview.
  • An offer is discussed by phone first, then issued online, contingent on background and reference checks — both are required for this position.
  • You can track status any time under My Job Applications in your account.

A primer on the actual work

Enough to write a credible application and hold an interview conversation. Source Water Protection sits in MDH's Environmental Health Division, under the Drinking Water Protection program.

Minnesotans drink from groundwater, lakes, and rivers. SWP works with communities to identify what threatens those sources and prevent problems before treatment is the only option. MDH names the risks specifically: unused wells, urban pollutants, agricultural nutrients, storage tanks, lawn chemicals, hazardous waste, and uncontrolled land development. In the Metro District, that means helping public water suppliers write and carry out wellhead protection plans and surface water intake plans, feeding into watershed and groundwater planning with state and local partners, and representing drinking water interests in Twin Cities regional water supply planning.

Decoding the posting

Terms that carry real consequences and aren't obvious coming from private practice.

Classified
Civil service. Competitive appointment, a probationary period, and meaningful job protection plus step-based salary progression once you're through it.
Unlimited
Permanent and ongoing — not grant-funded or time-limited. Worth noting, since a lot of public health roles are the opposite.
MAPE, unit 214
Minnesota Association of Professional Employees. Your pay, steps, and terms come from their contract rather than individual negotiation.
Nonexempt
Overtime-eligible, which is unusual at this salary and means hours past 40 are paid rather than absorbed.
Telework up to 50%
Requires living in Minnesota, or within 50 miles of the work location in a bordering state. Hopkins clears this comfortably.
Connect 700
A program for applicants with disabilities: apply online, then email the Job ID, working title, and your Proof of Eligibility Certificate to Laura Jones before the closing date. It routes you through a demonstration period rather than standard screening.
